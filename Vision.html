Who am I as a designer?

My Professional Identity and Vision - Empowering Neurodiversity

My vision is one heavily formed through my neurodivergent identity
and other own experiences. Some of my core design focuses stem
from activities that I enjoy doing, and I hope to follow and explore
these to develop myself and my skills even further.

Ensuring interactions with technologies and devices are meaningful and important to the user is very important to me. Especially when focusing on neurodiversity, designing empowering technologies that support users in self-development can prove substantial in long-term change. Neurodivergent individuals have strengths that do not always have the space, means, or visibility to develop. [2, 4] Ensuring these qualities have the tools to grow can prove extremely beneficial to future generations by providing neurodivergent individuals the means and skills for self-improvement and improved self-regulation. Companies are setting up Autism- and neurodiversity embassies to ensure their neurodiverse employees are properly supported, but these are mainly focused on raising awareness and improving work-related influences. These improvements can, for example, take the forms of modified communication styles, physical adjustments like headphones, and more. [8]

This vision partially stems from personal experience as a neurodivergent individual. I often struggle with activities and skills I notice my peers doing with ease. I sometimes tell others that the ASD and ADD in me tend to clash, but this is not necessarily a joke. It tends to be true at times; the ASD in me needs structure, but the ADD in me has trouble sticking to the structure. Examples of struggles include my planning skills and interpersonal communication. For both skills, I have followed several courses and skill training where this was either a main or side focus, and this has improved naturally over time. But both these struggles are still omnipresent in my work.
I was lucky to land an internship where I could perform research in this field, and this research informed me that I am not the only person who has difficulties with these skills. Yet, outside of studies into the origins of these struggles, I cannot find more than a few studies into supportive design considerations for a neurodivergent audience. [1, 3, 4, 6, 7]

Continuing on this, I am interested in seeing how supportive technologies may influence vulnerable audiences and their skills/needs. Are digital aspects more harmful or helpful, and how can we better design physical artefacts for self-development? In what ways could tools and services help or obstruct skill development in vulnerable individuals? And is it possible for tools to support positive development without reducing a vulnerable individual’s strengths? I believe that more user-involved studies can help gather insights into these questions and lead to more inclusive and supportive design for these individuals.

As a designer, I feel that it is up to me and my peers to provide a scientific and conceptual basis for products and services in this same direction. As such, I would ideally want to grow my portfolio with research and design probes stemming from this field, focusing my track towards design for behaviour change, innovative interactions, and vulnerable audiences. I am further very interested in research, and plan on following the (former) Constructive Design Research track in my continued master’s study. In this way, I hope to be able to provide meaningful research and design insights for supporting neurodiversity.
As a neurodivergent designer, I tend to be very research focused. I prefer working from knowledge and enjoy performing (exploratory) research to improve my understanding of any given project brief. This means I work hard to ensure that steps taken through my design process are properly supported by the knowledge and context of many underlying factors that might influence the topic or audience I am designing for.

My neurodivergent identity plays a large role in the design direction I tend to focus on. The majority of projects I have worked on have involved design for healthcare and the ethics hereof, exploring solutions for self-development and improving quality of life. Even if I have difficulties directly interacting with users by myself, I believe their perspectives are the most important in a project. In order to explore and analyse these user experiences, I have guided the methods of user involvement in all but one of my projects; both individual and group. I have a high understanding of ethics in research, and use research and my developments through several courses to inform new user interactions. I often look toward more novel means of user perspective while setting up these interactions, like design probes [9] and co-creation [5, 10], while ensuring the collected data remains relevant.

Because of my neurodivergent identity, I require structure to properly achieve my goals and prioritise tasks that I need to complete. Unfortunately, also because of my neurodivergent identity, I often struggle with creating structure and priorities for myself. This is an issue that I have been combating when working in individual projects. In group projects, I tend to look at my peers for help with this. My strengths lie in research and structured prototyping activities, involving inclusive- and experience design to ensure users remain at the heart of my approach. Low-Fi skills like sketching and storyboarding help me translate and communicate my concepts in early design stages, while struggles with mental processing time obstruct me while writing down my concepts more concrete in final project stages. I have more recently found my necessary working methods in physical notetaking, literature review and report sketching over digital work, and I hope to be able to improve this in my M1.1.
